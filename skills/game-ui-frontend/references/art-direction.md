# From a website brief to an art direction

Start with the visitor's decision. “Choose a pedal by ear” and “book a repair for a pedal I own” can come from the same maker and need different screens. Write one sentence in the client’s language that says what the visitor should notice, feel and do. Use it to decide where the page spends its visual energy.

Use the brief and this skill to choose the layout, controls, copy and motion. Discard conventional AI website defaults. For instance, a gig photographer's brief might call for choosing a band like a fighter. Open on a roster of real bands, show the selected concert photograph at full size, and put **Open the shoot** on a concert-ticket button. The roster comes from the selection study, the photographs from the work, and the ticket from the gig.

## Choose a reference for a reason

For a playable brief, start with the [game design philosophy](research/game-design-philosophy.md). Identify
the visitor's verb, the rule that makes the choice meaningful, the visible result and what can be carried
forward or tried again. The [print room](../templates/print-room.html) is a working example of a spatial
workbench with a startup and an exportable result.

Combine references where they solve different parts of the *same* problem. A roster can organize a catalog while a rendering study guides its product art. A tax form with a strong illustration brief might use the drawing style and keep every field in normal reading order. A short menu can get its entire direction from one object and one gesture.

## Compose the direction

- **Lead with the subject.** Name the real object, work sample or outcome that deserves the largest area. Give it a recognizable contour, crop and relationship to the title. A visitor should recognize the page’s purpose with the animation paused.
- **Draw the button at rest.** Give every action, including secondary choices, a visible surface around its label. A flat ticket in the venue's ink colour can do the whole job. Keep arrows and underlines off the button in every state. Show focus with a clear ring and selection through the plate's shape or fill.
- **Give lines a job.** A route line can connect two real destinations. An edge can show where a visitor can grab a tool. Omit ornamental thick or thin 3D lines, stacked bevels, wireframes and drifting connector trails used just to dress up a surface.
- **Write the actual reads.** Name what is visible on arrival, what changes when a visitor examines one choice, and what a deliberate action opens. Price, availability, errors and consequences belong at the decision they affect. Move process notes and further detail behind clearly named links or disclosures.
- **Remove decorative numbering and slashes.** Leave `/ 02`, `03 / SERVICES` and `01 / 03` off headings, cards and menu labels. Name the actual service, band or print. A real edition of 30 prints or “Step 2 of 3” during booking helps someone decide or finish a task. Drop tiny captions that repeat the title, label a section as a section, or invent a system status.
- **Keep useful words close.** Give each section one legible heading. Avoid giant background words, repeated display titles and fine print tucked against the section's bottom edge. Put the price beside the print and the access details beside the session time, at readable body size on desktop and phone.
- **Draw the transition.** Describe the starting pose, the first visible response, the settled view and the route back. Identify one thing that stays put so a visitor can compare successive choices. Give each movement a meaning, such as replacing the subject, revealing a surface or confirming a choice. Keep controls operable during the change.
- **Give the visitor the same decision on each device.** Describe the phone composition, the touch action that replaces hover and the still pose that carries selection with reduced motion. Use focus to reveal the same comparison data. Let an explicit action open detail.

In the response, lead with the design philosophy in the language of the brief. Walk through one visit using real labels, explain the signature control and name the game moment that informed it. These are design notes for the person building the site. Only visitor-facing copy belongs in the mockup.

## Two directions from the same print studio

**“Sell limited-run prints. People buy for the colour and paper.”** *Let someone handle a print before they buy it.* Start with a wall of recognizable compositions and one large sheet under raking light. Choosing **Tidepool** leaves the wall in place and brings its deckled edge, paper size, price and stock into view. **Inspect the paper** opens a true-colour close-up. **Add to bag** gives the sheet a short registration shift and updates the bag count. Use the roster’s relationship between thumbnail and portrait and the camera study’s change of framing. On a phone, keep the selected print and purchase facts together. Keep photographic colour accurate because it is a buying criterion.

**“Book a beginner screen-printing class in our studio.”** *Let a first-timer picture what they will make, then find a bench they can attend.* Lead with a finished two-colour print beside the inked screen. **Choose a session** reveals actual dates, duration, price, access information and places available. The selected date stays in view as the visitor reviews the reservation. Its confirmation can register a second ink layer over the first. Here Warframe informs how a visitor moves from a destination to its requirements. The paper, ink and language come from the studio. If there is only one location, a location map adds a decision the visitor never needed.
