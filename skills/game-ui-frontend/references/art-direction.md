# From a website brief to an art direction

Start with the visitor's decision. “Choose a pedal by ear” and “book a repair for a pedal I own” can come from the same maker and need different screens. Write one sentence in the client’s language that says what the visitor should notice, feel and do. Use it to decide where the page spends its visual energy.

## Design from one real example

Take one actual shoot, order, session or job from the brief. What matters about it when seen alongside the others? What becomes visible or possible only when someone acts on it? Use those answers to decide what occupies the screen and how the visitor moves through it. Look up the game moment that solves the same interaction problem. A peg can keep a print in its place. A handoff can show exactly which document the next worker received. Choose details for what they let the visitor understand or do.

**A photographer who hangs prints after each walk.** Suppose neighbouring pictures tell the story of one street. Open on the photographer's hanging board with the supplied shots in walk order. A landscape print takes up more of the line than a portrait. Leave each image unobstructed and give its paper edge a clear focus treatment. Choosing a print moves the view towards that very print.

Open the full frame. A suggested close-up might reveal a face reflected in a shop window. Let someone enlarge the photo, inspect the lettering at the other end of the street, and fit the print again. A full-resolution image in a keyboard-scrollable view can do this with one zoom control. Return to the same peg, scroll position and focus. On a phone, run the hanging sequence down the page with generous targets. With reduced motion, switch framing directly. For a large archive, hang the current series together, keep every series reachable and load the full-resolution file when its print opens.

The picture selection in *Life is Strange: Before the Storm* keeps the choices in place as a white frame moves across them. The *Sifu* evidence-board still shows separate pieces sharing a surface. In this brief the photographer supplied the hanging ritual. A photographer who simply says “I shoot in pairs so people can spot what changed” has also given you enough to invent two prints on one peg, with a shared point to inspect. The mount and the move into the image are original design choices. If clients arrive to book school portraits, put the session choices and dates within reach on arrival. **A SaaS team whose agents turn changes into release notes.** Find out what a customer gives the product, where the work changes hands, and what the customer keeps. Suppose a collector reads merged changes and an editor returns a draft with source references. Let the visitor select two changes and choose **Draft release notes**. Keep the selected titles in view. As the collector finishes, its findings appear in the editor's input tray. They stay available to inspect while the draft takes shape. Choose a sentence in the finished draft to pick out the change that supports it. Remove one change and run the sample again. The corresponding sentence should disappear. That difference gives the visitor a way to judge the product.

Use *Warframe*'s in-place preparation to inform how someone inspects and launches a job. The product determines whether workers start together or one waits for another's output. In a connected product, follow the same run through its reported events to its result. If a handoff fails, show which input reached which worker. In a public demo, label the sample at the launch control and let its output follow the chosen inputs. A team selling a hands-off service might prefer one inspectable past run and a booking link. If the product instead reserves studio time, spend the interaction on finding an open slot and seeing what the booking includes. [Warframe mission preparation](research/warframe-navigation.md)

Before committing to a direction, change one fact from the brief. If changing which photos belong together or what the software produces leaves the same layout, gesture and result, revisit the interaction. Keep this test in the design process. The site itself only needs the words that help the visitor act.

If customers choose which agent runs a job, study Kirby's rider screen. Moving through the grid reveals capabilities, choosing a rider changes the ready screen, and starting remains a separate action. Try those inputs on the site and inspect the queue after each. If the product always assigns its own agents, give the visitor control over the job inputs and let them inspect the resulting handoff. For a playable brief, start with the [game design philosophy](research/game-design-philosophy.md). Identify
the visitor's verb, the rule that makes the choice meaningful, the visible result and what can be carried
forward or tried again. The [complete essay readings](research/essay-readings.md) connect each film to a decision it can help the visitor make. The [print room](../templates/print-room.html) is a working example of a spatial
workbench with a startup and an exportable result.

Combine references where they solve different parts of the *same* problem. A roster can organize a catalog while a rendering study guides its product art. A tax form with a strong illustration brief might use the drawing style and keep every field in normal reading order. A short menu can get its entire direction from one object and one gesture.

## Compose the direction

- **Lead with the subject.** Name the real object, work sample or outcome that deserves the largest area. Give it a recognizable contour, crop and relationship to the title. A visitor should recognize the page’s purpose with the animation paused.
- **Draw the button at rest.** Give every action, including secondary choices, a visible surface around its label. A flat ticket in the venue's ink colour can do the whole job. Keep its words clean. One cursor can pick a ticket out of the rack. A bearing belongs on a plan where it can actually point towards the chosen venue. Keep keyboard focus visible when it moves away from the booked ticket.
- **Give lines a job.** A route line can connect two real destinations. An edge can show where a visitor can grab a tool. Omit ornamental thick or thin 3D lines, stacked bevels, wireframes and drifting connector trails used just to dress up a surface.
- **Write the actual reads.** Name what is visible on arrival, what changes when a visitor examines one choice, and what a deliberate action opens. Price, availability, errors and consequences belong at the decision they affect. Move process notes and further detail behind clearly named links or disclosures.
- **Remove decorative numbering and slashes.** Leave `/ 02`, `03 / SERVICES` and `01 / 03` off headings, cards and menu labels. Name the actual service, band or print. A real edition of 30 prints or “Step 2 of 3” during booking helps someone decide or finish a task. Drop tiny captions that repeat the title, label a section as a section, or invent a system status.
- **Keep useful words close.** Give each section one legible heading. Avoid giant background words, repeated display titles and fine print tucked against the section's bottom edge. Put the price beside the print and the access details beside the session time, at readable body size on desktop and phone.
- **Draw the transition.** Describe the starting pose, the first visible response, the settled view and the route back. Identify one thing that stays put so a visitor can compare successive choices. Give each movement a meaning, such as replacing the subject, revealing a surface or confirming a choice. Keep controls operable during the change.
- **Give the visitor the same decision on each device.** Describe the phone composition, the touch action that replaces hover and the still pose that carries selection with reduced motion. Use focus to reveal the same comparison data. Let an explicit action open detail.

For a direction request, walk through one visit using real labels and explain the game moment behind the signature control. For a build, implement that visit and give the user the working result along with the source for the design decision. Keep these notes in the handoff.

## Two directions from the same print studio

**“Sell limited-run prints. People buy for the colour and paper.”** *Let someone handle a print before they buy it.* Start with a wall of recognizable compositions and one large sheet under raking light. Choosing **Tidepool** leaves the wall in place and brings its deckled edge, paper size, price and stock into view. **Inspect the paper** opens a true-colour close-up. **Add to bag** gives the sheet a short registration shift and updates the bag count. Use the roster’s relationship between thumbnail and portrait and the camera study’s change of framing. On a phone, keep the selected print and purchase facts together. Keep photographic colour accurate because it is a buying criterion.

**“Book a beginner screen-printing class in our studio.”** *Let a first-timer picture what they will make, then find a bench they can attend.* Lead with a finished two-colour print beside the inked screen. **Choose a session** reveals actual dates, duration, price, access information and places available. The selected date stays in view as the visitor reviews the reservation. Its confirmation can register a second ink layer over the first. Here Warframe informs how a visitor moves from a destination to its requirements. The paper, ink and language come from the studio. If there is only one location, a location map adds a decision the visitor never needed.
