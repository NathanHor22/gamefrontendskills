# Reading the seventeen video essays

These are the distinct essays supplied for this study, in the order they were first shared. Together their published runtimes are 7 hours, 14 minutes and 35 seconds. The pause-menu video was shared twice. The separate 150-game PSP compilation is outside this reading.

For each essay we read the complete available English caption track in sequence, including corrections, examples that complicate the opening premise, guest passages, promotions and farewells. The count is the number of nonempty caption cues in the English track used here. It measures coverage of the text. A cue can contain several sentences, part of a sentence, a sound label or a mistranscription. The studies use original analysis and timed links to the recordings. They identify what specific footage shows and what the speaker infers. Edited footage seldom reveals the physical input or the instant the next control accepts it.

Across the six authored and eleven automatic tracks, that is 8,850 cues. We also checked uncertain passages against source audio or visible words. In the genre history, a second transcription places the final farewell around 2:17:47. The credits continue over music. In Pumpo's closing montage the sung words are still uncertain. In the Kirby essay a member's on-screen name resolves a mistake in both readings of the sound. [Genre history's closing passage](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dbgdi7BzMR0&t=8232s) · [Pumpo's outro](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZCfi-cOk9VI&t=1170s) · [Kirby's contributor roster](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mrGQhOqty6A&t=783s)

One omission matters even in a supplied subtitle track. During Riot's spectator example, the captions label the match commentary as a sound. A second pass over the audio recovered a call to disengage and a reaction to damage. That gives the designer a concrete question about the spectator's screen. Can someone tell what just happened before the commentary has moved on to the next play? Individual player names in the call remain unclear. [Riot's match excerpt, 6:37–6:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=397s)

### Using a finding

Start with the real decision in the brief. For “let people try an agent that edits release notes,” the Kirby study gives three observable states to prototype. Focusing an agent reveals its capabilities. Choosing it puts its name on the job. **Run job** starts work and produces a draft. Move focus between two agents, choose one, leave the screen and reopen it. If the queue gained a job before anyone pressed Run, the transition has communicated the wrong consequence. For a site that only shows completed work, the useful verb may be inspecting a result. That brief has no reason to inherit the launch step.
