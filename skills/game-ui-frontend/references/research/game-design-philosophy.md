# What makes a game worth playing again?

Give the player something worth finding out. Let them form an expectation, act on it, read the consequence and try a different idea. The pleasure might be anticipation before a jump, recognition when a familiar fighter appears, or surprise at the result of combining two materials. Name which pleasure a particular design offers. A source can show a player making a choice. Whether visitors to a new site will enjoy its equivalent is a hypothesis to test.

## Read the argument alongside the screen

The [reading index for all seventeen essays](essay-readings.md) lists each source, its English caption track and the question it can answer. Follow an individual study for the author's reasoning, the behaviour visible on screen and where a web adaptation could fail.

Keep the author's explanation and the visible evidence together. At a named playhead, identify what the player needs to know, where the interface puts it, what their input changes and what persists. Explain why that combination could work for the visitor. Use an adjacent frame, another choice or a return trip to test the mechanism. If the narrator supplies a motive and the picture only shows the layout, attribute the motive to the narrator. The visitor's version is a design hypothesis until someone can perform it.

## Two proposed transfers

**“Let customers explore two-colour printing and take a proof away.”** Open on an original workbench with one inked screen suspended over paper. A visitor can enter the bench immediately. Let the registration corner be recognizable in both the wide view and the close view. Choosing an ink changes the swatch. Pulling a proof changes the paper. Keep the same sheet visible as the visitor changes one ink and tries again, so the overlap teaches something about the process. A downloadable proof carries the actual choices out of the experience. In the direct controls, **Pull proof** performs the same operation as a gesture on the press. This transfers advance information, a reversible experiment and a meaningful outcome into a spatial scene.

**“Help people buy a reissue from our record label.”** A startup can begin at the first meaningful choice. Show the sleeves with their spines facing the visitor. **Browse releases** opens them immediately, and choosing a sleeve brings its cover, artist and price into view. **Hear a sample** belongs to the selected record, and browsing can continue while it plays. Someone arriving on a specific release starts with that sleeve already open. The joy here is recognition and discovery. Listening and buying should remain available without completing a dexterity challenge.

In the handoff, name what the visitor controls, what the system changes in response, what survives leaving and returning, and what useful outcome they carry away. To test the proposal, ask someone to try one alternative and explain what changed. If they can only describe the animation, revisit the rule or the feedback.

**What happened when we tried it.** In the [working print room](../../templates/print-room.html), the first version illustrated the squeegee but required a separate slider to move it. The critic tried dragging the object itself. That failed a reasonable prediction about the room, so the revised scene maps the drag to the press bed. On release the ink stays where it was laid down, even if the blade travels back. The slider and one-pass action operate the same press. The reviewer changed ink, pulled another proof and verified that each downloaded PNG matched the proof displayed. This tests whether the mechanic does what it promises. Visitor enjoyment remains a separate question.
