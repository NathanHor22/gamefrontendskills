# Choosing an interaction from the history of game genres

Mark Brown’s *The History of Every Video Game Genre* asks what kinds of decisions games have offered over time. He selected up to 200 popular games per year on Backloggd for 1975–2025, yielding 9,902 games, and worked from Wikipedia genre descriptions. His counts reflect that selection, including its language and popularity biases. They cannot establish that one control is intrinsically better or that nobody else was making a kind of game. [Original film](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dbgdi7BzMR0) · [Brown’s source notes](https://gamemakerstoolkit.com/sources-the-history-of-every-video-game-genre/)

## What changes when you alter one rule?

**Replace the opponent.** At 14:45 *Breakout* shows a paddle below a partially cleared wall. A gap stays open after a brick disappears, so the board itself records progress. At 21:07 *Space Invaders* shows rows above the player and shelters in between. Six seconds later the film cuts back to a fresh, stationary wall of bricks to explain the connection. Brown attributes the shift to asking what would happen if those blocks could act back. When a SaaS brief says “spin up agents”, establish whether the work advances while the visitor waits. On a running queue, progress should follow the jobs even when the visitor puts the pointer down. On a planning board, an unconfirmed arrangement should hold still. Test by pausing for a job’s expected completion time with a draft still open. Can the visitor tell which state advanced and whether the draft survived? [Breakout, 14:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dbgdi7BzMR0&t=885s) · [The two boards, 21:07–21:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dbgdi7BzMR0&t=1267s)

**Decide who supplies the puzzle.** At 1:00:05 *Soko-ban* has a bounded floor plan and a fixed cluster of pieces. At 1:00:26 *Lode Runner* places its character among platforms, gaps and ladders. Brown says it also let players build stages. At 1:01:05 the *Tetris* example has a live piece, a partly filled well and a queue of upcoming shapes. These produce different expectations for a textile-design site. An artist can give a visitor a specific pattern to reconstruct, let them author a tile and share it, or keep dealing pieces for a short challenge. Ask which artefact someone should leave with. For a pattern editor, share a tile with two custom colours and open it on another device. A newly generated starter tile would fail even if its palette looked right. [Puzzle sequence, 1:00:05–1:01:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dbgdi7BzMR0&t=3605s)

**A tool determines the next reasonable input.** In *Dune II* at 1:07:36 the right panel identifies a soldier and asks for a target. At 1:07:44 that same panel shows concrete and asks for a building location. Both views preserve the terrain, expose a Cancel control and change what clicking the same ground means. Brown’s larger point is that deliberation now takes place in a world moving in real time. For a warehouse-planning product, keep the floor fixed while **Inspect pallet** and **Place rack** change what the next tap will do. With a rack selected, tap an occupied bay, cancel and select that bay for inspection. The rejected placement should leave the existing pallet, selection and count intact. A reticle alone cannot explain why the action changed. [Dune II panels, 1:07:36–1:07:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dbgdi7BzMR0&t=4056s)

**Change whose outcome counts.** Brown says *The Sims* began as an interior-design idea in which the inhabitants would evaluate the furniture. At 1:16:59 a kitchen and people’s status share the screen. At 1:17:06 someone stands beside the fridge. By 1:17:16 the same room is on fire and figures react. These frames establish the change in circumstances. They give no evidence that opening the fridge caused the fire. Brown credits Roxy Wolosenko and Claire Curtin with pushing the people and relationships to the foreground. In a theatre set planner the same objects can serve two briefs. A designer assessing the composition needs the audience view. A stage manager checking a set change needs the route between the flats. Move one flat across an exit, then switch views. Both must retain its position, and the second must expose the obstruction. [The Sims kitchen, 1:16:59–1:17:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dbgdi7BzMR0&t=4619s)

**Switching viewpoints can carry the whole interaction.** At 1:59:58 *Five Nights at Freddy’s* shows the desk at 5 AM with very little power left. At 2:00:00 the west-hall feed and a small camera map fill the view. By 2:00:04 the office is dark. The edit supports the visible sequence. It does not isolate whether that particular camera switch exhausted the power. Brown uses the game after *Amnesia* to show how removing movement, combat and inventory can concentrate attention on observation. For a wildlife project, let someone switch between a nest view and a hide overlooking its feeding ground. Preserve the selected observation time so they can compare the same event. Test with a feed gap. A labelled empty interval is a more useful answer than silently switching to unrelated footage. [Office and monitor, 1:59:58–2:00:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dbgdi7BzMR0&t=7198s)

**Carry the reusable rule, and choose where it stops.** Brown describes *Spelunky* combining *Mario*’s platform movement with *Hack*’s changing layouts and permanent resets. The montage shows *Super Mario Bros.* at 2:03:21 and *Spelunky Classic*’s cave at 2:03:35–2:03:44. Those frames show traversal within a cave. The restart rule comes from the narration. A record shop can use this idea to deal a fresh listening selection on request. Keep a visitor’s saved record and shareable album address stable across deals. Test by saving the third record, shuffling twice, then opening its link in a fresh session. If its identity changes, the randomisation has reached content that should persist. [Mario and Spelunky, 2:03:21–2:03:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dbgdi7BzMR0&t=7401s)

## Where the history pushes back

Brown offers several reasons a graph can tempt a designer into the wrong conclusion. Run-and-gun releases fall as first-person shooters rise, yet he points out that the two histories largely involve different development communities. Independent interactive fiction barely registers at all. Japanese adventure, visual novel and dating games suffer from a language and popularity filter. A zero in the top 200 for puzzle games in 2002 coexists with releases outside the cutoff. The subsequent return of puzzles also has an economic explanation. Smaller teams could sell work through casual portals, mobile stores and digital console or PC shops at prices that made little sense on a boxed retail shelf. Those are explanations about who could make, sell and discover a game. They tell a designer little about what a particular visitor can operate today. [Shooter caveat, 52:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dbgdi7BzMR0&t=3140s) · [Language limits, 1:36:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dbgdi7BzMR0&t=5780s) · [Shelf space to digital distribution, 1:50:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dbgdi7BzMR0&t=6630s)

The late chapters complicate the value of adding more play. Mods and editors let players isolate one promising activity. Story games can instead remove entire classes of action, and *Dear Esther* leaves a stretch of coastline available to explore on foot. The idle-game examples began as criticism before becoming attractions themselves. *Minecraft* offers different relationships to the same building material through its creative and survival modes. A cosy experience reduces the cost of failure. A social hangout leaves attention free for another person. The closing roll call includes education, quiz, exercise, location-based work and FMV, whose status as a genre Brown questions. At 2:17:12 six stores give *Prince of Persia: The Lost Crown* six different labels. When a client asks for “an RPG portfolio”, ask what the visitor will keep or change after choosing a project. A genre name cannot answer that question on their behalf. ## Build from the brief

**A record-cutting studio says customers keep sending tracks that won’t fit the side they’ve ordered.** Start on the cutting desk with the customer’s tracks and the two sides of their chosen format. Pick up a track by its title and place it on Side A or B. A moving endpoint on that side’s groove and a readable time show exactly where the track lands. Let someone select a track and press **Place on Side B** on a phone or keyboard. Take the duration limit from the studio’s actual quote. Carry the accepted sequence into the enquiry. The beat target supplies the idea of an event meeting a fixed line. The work at the document desk supplies the idea of judging several visible facts together. The groove, timing display and two-side assignment are our design.

Test with a fixture in which the studio allows 12:00 per side. Put 7:00 and 6:00 on Side A. The groove crosses the limit by 1:00. Move the second track to Side B. The enquiry should now retain the two titles, their sides, and totals of 7:00 and 6:00. Someone using reduced motion should get the same endpoint and result immediately.

**A neighbourhood archive says the important part is hearing two residents describe the same corner.** Open on the corner already visible, with the two recordings mounted on that location. Picking a speaker brings their photograph and indexed transcript into focus while the street stays in place. **Hear the other account** goes to the other resident’s passage about that corner. It takes an explicit play action to start sound. Brown’s narrative chapters suggest removing the puzzle, and the *Déjà Vu* sequence shows why the selected place can remain visible while the available action changes. The scene is useful because it preserves what the two speakers have in common.

Test with two recordings whose accounts of the corner start at different timestamps. Switching accounts should land on each recording’s own indexed passage. Browser Back should restore the speaker, passage and corner. If the archive’s real priority changes to licensing one image, replace the paired listening action with a rights enquiry that carries that image’s identifier.
