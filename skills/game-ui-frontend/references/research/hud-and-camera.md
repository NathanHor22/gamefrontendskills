# What the visitor can read from here

Game Maker’s Toolkit examines two decisions that a website designer makes together. A HUD determines which facts reach the player during an action. A camera determines what the player can already see. Both English subtitle tracks were read in sequence through their final remarks, including the HUD film’s closing puzzle recommendation. The two films have 244 and 180 text cues respectively. The frame observations below come from decoded footage. Brown’s account supplies the development history. The website behaviours are design proposals.

## What happens next, and who makes it happen?

In the HUD film, *Slay the Spire* and *Shovel Knight: Specter of Torment* answer two different questions. Brown says early *Slay the Spire* players had too little information to choose intelligently between blocking and attacking. An enemy’s next intent belongs above that enemy, since it remains true while the player considers different cards. In *Specter of Torment*, a changed character pose told players very little about the destination of a possible dash. At 3:54 a lantern hangs between the character and a ledge. At 4:04 a diagonal streak cuts across the lantern with the character on the other side. The latter sequence illustrates a conditional preview attached to the object the player can act on. Its direction matters as much as its presence. [Enemy intent, 2:23–2:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=143s) · [Lantern demonstration, 3:54–4:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=234s)

For a service that launches workers, ask the owner what is already scheduled and what the visitor is free to change. A pending run can show its actual start time on the job. Focusing **Try two workers** can preview which queued jobs would start together. Confirming starts the job only if the product offers that action. Test with one queued job, one blocked job and two free workers. If the blocked job appears to start in the preview, the control is giving the visitor a false model. Verify the same preview on focus and tap.

## Information has a reading order

At 7:10 Mark Brown introduces Zach Gage’s idea of successive reads. In *SpellTower*, letters get the first read because they are what the player uses. Nearby danger and the bonus letters that can clear a line earn the second. A rule that applies only when making a longer word can wait for a closer look. By 7:52, a card stands out above the hand in *Hearthstone*. At 7:55 the card has gone and two pieces carry bright impact marks. Importance can change during the same turn. [GMTK, 7:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=430s) · [Hearthstone sequence, 7:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=472s) · [Zach Gage’s illustrated talk](https://stfj.net/DesigningForSubwayLegibility/)

At 9:40, *Dead Space* shows a store panel in the space in front of the character. The heading and rows are still recognizable as a shop. At 12:00, *Reigns* puts the decision on a card with four resources above it. The edit at 12:10 highlights a version of those resources with explicit quantities. Treat this as a comparison in the essay. It makes a good design question: does the visitor need to know **which** outcome changes, **in which direction**, or **by exactly how much**? [Store panel, 9:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=580s) · [Reigns comparison, 12:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=720s)

**Website decision.** In a furniture configurator, the grain of the chosen wood can be the first read. Put its name and the resulting price beside the swatches. Selecting oak should show the actual revised total before **Add to basket**. The joinery diagram and care instructions can open under **Construction and care**. A beautifully drawn material sample still needs a text name when colour, screen size or vision makes two finishes hard to distinguish.

**Decide what someone can afford to miss.** Brown opens with the late HUD work on *The Last of Us*. He then gives two costs of removing information. A player forced to count shots has to remember a number the interface could readily provide. Someone who misses the small trespassing label in *Hitman* can keep moving under the wrong assumption. The 6:42 frame isolates the original white label, and the 6:50 comparison adds a yellow one beside it. Brown describes the animated map warning in the sequel and the sound cue in the third game. In *Ghost of Tsushima*, drawing the sword supplies a natural moment to show health. On a reservation desk, give an expired hold one visible state change and leave the status readable when the animation ends. Let the visitor return to the seat map with their selection in view. Test with audio muted and the tab briefly in the background. [Alert comparison, 6:42–6:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=402s) · [Timing a health display, 8:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=500s)

**A physical instrument has an opportunity cost.** *Dead Space* and *Metroid Prime* place readouts on equipment. *Alien: Isolation* goes further. The handheld tracker fills the near foreground at 10:33. By 10:41 another tool occupies that space. Brown explains that using one gives up immediate use of the other. A photographer’s loupe makes a similar exchange meaningful. Hold or toggle it to inspect grain while the surrounding contact sheet stays in place. Remove the loupe to compare whole frames again. Give it a latched button for touch and keyboard. If toggling it only adds glass reflections, remove it. [Tracker sequence, 10:22–10:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=622s)

**Choose how far a preview should reach.** Brown gives *The Last of Us Part II*’s unintelligible whistles as a deliberate loss of knowledge, and *Reigns* as a case where exposing exact amounts changed players’ priorities. In *Peggle* the normal dotted path stops at the first collision. The frames at 13:21 and 13:26 show the aim and the lit peg. At 13:33 a guide bends past the first contact. The extra prediction is a power-up. Brown’s patent aside is followed by another use for adjustable information, the optional racing line. An interactive exhibition can let someone guess where a reflected beam will land and then reveal the path. In a paid print order the exact total and delivery choice belong before commitment, every time. [Whistles and Reigns, 11:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=660s) · [Aim and extended guide, 13:21–13:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=801s)

**Let the drawing change the rule.** Brown closes with *Into the Breach*. At 14:59 an action panel and highlighted cells share a small board. Subset Games cut proposals whose chains or area of effect were too hard to communicate there. Brown also connects *Hearthstone*’s seven-minion limit to available screen space. His final recommendation, *Room to Grow*, shows a cactus using a wall to move its own body. The board lets the player see why that new rule works. For a seating planner, prototype a group move on the smallest supported screen. Ask someone to point to every seat it will change before committing. If the preview cannot answer, offer smaller moves the visitor can inspect individually. [Tactics board, 14:43–15:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=883s) · [Closing puzzle, 16:11–16:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=971s)

## A closer crop changes what the interface owes the visitor

The camera essay diagrams distance and intimacy at 3:30. At 5:10 its *God of War* comparison shows several figures and the space between them. At 5:35 Kratos’s shoulder occupies much of the near frame beside Atreus. The first view makes relative position easy to read. In the second, a face and gesture are easier to inspect, while some surroundings fall outside the frame. Santa Monica Studio’s combat designer describes how the team adapted its systems for the closer camera. [GMTK, 3:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bHdi5Ar8GXw&t=210s) · [Comparison, 5:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bHdi5Ar8GXw&t=310s) · [Mihir Sheth at GDC](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1026085/Evolving-Combat-in-God-of)

**Website decision.** On a furniture page, a wide shot establishes a chair’s size beside a table. **Inspect the joint** can cut to a close-up while the model name, chosen finish and **Back to chair** stay visible. Return to the same chair, finish and scroll position. A pair of art-directed stills can carry both views. On a narrow screen, place the current choice and return control next to the image. In reduced motion, cut directly between framings. Visitor scrolling stays under their control.

**When the visitor’s verb changes, reconsider the framing.** In *Batman: Arkham Asylum*, the sampled corridor footage follows close behind Batman. Brown explains that combat pulls the camera back to frame the opponent, and that following a target can cause a disorienting swing. For a 3D workshop, a bench-wide view lets the visitor locate tools. A close view lets them operate one. Anchor the chosen tool on entry and restore the bench on exit. Test whether someone who has just inspected a tool can find its neighbour again. [GMTK, 8:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bHdi5Ar8GXw&t=489s)

## Who chooses the next view?

*Resident Evil 2* gives the director control over what Leon can see. At 1:55 the corridor walls converge, at 2:03 Brown outlines the far window, and at 2:11 the shot puts the viewer outside the window looking in at Leon. At 2:27 an elevated angle reveals a body in the foreground. The cuts change what the player suspects about a familiar hallway. Brown contrasts this with the remake’s view from behind Leon and with *Tomb Raider* framing Lara’s face during a climb. In a photography archive, an authored cut from a wide room to an individual face can tell a story. Give someone looking for a particular gig a direct route to that frame. A suspenseful blind turn has value only for the visitor who chose to follow the story. [Hallway sequence, 1:55–2:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bHdi5Ar8GXw&t=115s) · [Remake and Lara comparison, 3:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bHdi5Ar8GXw&t=180s)

Camera distance changes the underlying work too. Brown describes *God of War* reducing off-screen enemy aggression, drawing Kratos toward nearby targets and limiting how high a target can rise. Direction cues and Atreus’s warnings carry information lost at the edge. The frames alone establish the crop. The hidden adjustments come from the commentary. Brown would widen the view dynamically in crowded encounters. In *Arkham Asylum*, compare Batman filling the left foreground at 8:15 with the larger group visible at 8:20. At 8:57 a cover view looks around a corner, at 9:03 a perch reveals the room, and at 9:10 the vent fills the image. Each answers a different spatial question. The footage is an edited comparison and gives no exact animation duration or hover behaviour. [Combat accommodations, 6:30–7:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bHdi5Ar8GXw&t=390s) · [Batman’s views, 8:15–9:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bHdi5Ar8GXw&t=495s)

The film opens with composition in *Shadow of the Colossus* and closes with alternatives to any single camera rule. *Mirror’s Edge* uses bodily cues and a wider field of view. *ARMS* uses the approaching fists to help communicate distance. *Skate* follows the board, and *Firewatch* lets a first-person character act with expressive hands. At 11:08 the board and ground sit prominently in frame, and at 11:15 the view already looks down a wooden stairway. Brown ends by asking readers to remember a camera that got in their way. On a mobile instrument builder, let a visitor select a tuning peg, read its pitch, change it, then compare it with the peg next door. Ask them to do the sequence twice with reduced motion. A crop that hides the active peg’s identity or forces them to search the whole instrument on return has failed the task. [Closing counterexamples, 10:35–11:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bHdi5Ar8GXw&t=635s)
