# What the visitor can read from here

Game Maker’s Toolkit examines two decisions that a website designer makes together. A HUD determines which facts reach the player during an action. A camera determines what the player can already see. These notes use the creator’s chapter markers, storyboard samples, decoded footage and selected passages transcribed from the original audio. The playheads locate the examples. The web behaviors are proposals.

## Information has a reading order

At 7:10 Mark Brown introduces Zach Gage’s idea of successive reads. In *SpellTower*, letters get the first read because they are what the player uses. Nearby danger and the bonus letters that can clear a line earn the second. A rule that applies only when making a longer word can wait for a closer look. By 7:52, a card stands out above the hand in *Hearthstone*. At 7:55 the card has gone and two pieces carry bright impact marks. Importance can change during the same turn. [GMTK, 7:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=430s) · [Hearthstone sequence, 7:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=472s) · [Zach Gage’s illustrated talk](https://stfj.net/DesigningForSubwayLegibility/)

At 9:40, *Dead Space* shows a store panel in the space in front of the character. The heading and rows are still recognizable as a shop. At 12:00, *Reigns* puts the decision on a card with four resources above it. The edit at 12:10 highlights a version of those resources with explicit quantities. Treat this as a comparison in the essay. It makes a good design question: does the visitor need to know **which** outcome changes, **in which direction**, or **by exactly how much**? [Store panel, 9:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=580s) · [Reigns comparison, 12:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Bv45aPMGyI&t=720s)

**Website decision.** In a furniture configurator, the grain of the chosen wood can be the first read. Put its name and the resulting price beside the swatches. Selecting oak should show the actual revised total before **Add to basket**. The joinery diagram and care instructions can open under **Construction and care**. A beautifully drawn material sample still needs a text name when colour, screen size or vision makes two finishes hard to distinguish.

## A closer crop changes what the interface owes the visitor

The camera essay diagrams distance and intimacy at 3:30. At 5:10 its *God of War* comparison shows several figures and the space between them. At 5:35 Kratos’s shoulder occupies much of the near frame beside Atreus. The first view makes relative position easy to read. In the second, a face and gesture are easier to inspect, while some surroundings fall outside the frame. Santa Monica Studio’s combat designer describes how the team adapted its systems for the closer camera. [GMTK, 3:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bHdi5Ar8GXw&t=210s) · [Comparison, 5:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bHdi5Ar8GXw&t=310s) · [Mihir Sheth at GDC](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1026085/Evolving-Combat-in-God-of)

**Website decision.** On a furniture page, a wide shot establishes a chair’s size beside a table. **Inspect the joint** can cut to a close-up while the model name, chosen finish and **Back to chair** stay visible. Return to the same chair, finish and scroll position. A pair of art-directed stills can carry both views. On a narrow screen, place the current choice and return control next to the image. In reduced motion, cut directly between framings. Visitor scrolling stays under their control.

**When the visitor’s verb changes, reconsider the framing.** In *Batman: Arkham Asylum*, the sampled corridor footage follows close behind Batman. Brown explains that combat pulls the camera back to frame the opponent, and that following a target can cause a disorienting swing. For a 3D workshop, a bench-wide view lets the visitor locate tools. A close view lets them operate one. Anchor the chosen tool on entry and restore the bench on exit. Test whether someone who has just inspected a tool can find its neighbour again.
