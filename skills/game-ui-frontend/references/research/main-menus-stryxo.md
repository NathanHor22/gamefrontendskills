# How a main menu introduces a place

Stryxo’s *i love main menus* asks whether the opening can tell you how a game feels while you find the action you came for. Its first impatient player wants to change the volume, motion blur or subtitles. Its Kingdom Hearts example is a viewer who has never played the game but already feels something for its starting island. Both are audiences for the same screen. ## Follow the selection through the opening

**Where can I go, and what can I do there?** At 9:23 the wide red Story column in *Mirror’s Edge* contains three actions. A white strip picks out Continue Game. At 9:24 Race occupies the width and the strip picks out Speed Run. At 9:26 Options holds Video, Audio, Controls and Game Settings. Extras follows at 9:29. The other section names survive each change on narrow spines. Quit Game and Friends stay in the lower corner. There are two levels of selection in a single view. One red surface answers “which family?”, one white surface answers “which action?”. The captures establish those states. They leave the initiating device and original travel time open. [Menu sequence, 9:23–9:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EoiC3LhPchA&t=563s)

For a photographer whose prints hang by show, give each show its own hanging sleeve. Opening one reveals real photographs while the neighbouring show names remain legible. A second, contained control opens the chosen photograph at viewing size. Try switching shows midway through the sleeve opening, then opening a picture. Its show, image and Back destination should all agree. On a phone where all the spines cannot fit, keep the active show name on the sleeve and offer a single return to the show rack.

**Who owns this screen?** Stryxo likes the corporate look of *DOOM* and dislikes that quality in later Xbox dashboards. His own examples explain the difference. At 9:48 DOOM's active save slot shows a preview and campaign information. At 9:52 a settings category reveals its values alongside. Later, a Windows-like grid puts games, services and promotions into adjacent rectangles. Stryxo's speculation about who paid for the space is a joke. The useful question for a build is whose decision receives the best space. In a mission-control website for an agent service, the operator's selected run can own the central instrument. Show its actual work, state and available intervention there. Ask someone to find their failed run on arrival and identify what they can retry. An unrelated promotion occupying that instrument would break the fiction and obstruct the task. ## Qualifications that change the recommendation

Stryxo sets aside publisher logos and trailers at the start. His impatient visitor wants volume, subtitles or motion settings before play. The quieter *Kingdom Hearts* example matters precisely because it works for him with zero play experience. Goodsprings in *Fallout: New Vegas* illustrates how a small starting place teaches the larger world. The remake comparison is about fans forming different attachments to a recurring motif. The imagined *Anthem* menu tests what a lavish opening can buy. The underlying game still has to earn the investment. His aside about finding poor-quality footage occurs after the *Darkness* and *Riddick* comparison. Precise timing would need better footage. [Opening and first place, 0:19–3:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EoiC3LhPchA&t=19s) · [The hypothetical menu, 3:34–4:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EoiC3LhPchA&t=214s)

The invented *Half-Life 2* commenter and *Croc* aside set up an overlooked return cue. Stryxo's month with *Final Fantasy VII Rebirth* explains when a picture of the last location is insufficient. Recalling *where* you stopped differs from recalling *why* an earlier event matters now. Give an interrupted multi-stage task a short account of the actual last decision when it changes the next one. The *City Escape* rails, hazard and score illustrate interdependence. The later claim that the Nemesis system alone explains *Shadow of Mordor* is expressly retracted as bait, and he finds value in *Anthem*'s music as well as its flight. The nostalgia of *Mirror’s Edge*, the frightening PS2 error and the preference for the 3DS shop all belong to this speaker. ## Put the opening on trial

- **First visit, muted.** Show someone a cold opening. Ask where they think they are, what they can do, and have them do it immediately. If they need a paragraph to discover that the painted radio can tune a station, change its shape, focus or visible verb.
- **Return after an interruption.** Select a particular programme, leave the site and return. Check that the opening restores the actual programme and that changing the saved choice changes what comes back. A random background can set the mood but cannot remind anyone what they did.
- **Interrupt the handoff.** Activate one choice, immediately use Back, then choose its neighbour. The label, selected object, destination and keyboard focus should agree. With reduced motion the same destination should be evident in a cut.
- **Remove a flourish.** Mute the sound, freeze the backdrop or remove the ornament. Ask what information disappeared. Keep a purposeful atmospheric detail when it makes the subject recognisable. Redraw anything that competes with the visitor’s first action.
