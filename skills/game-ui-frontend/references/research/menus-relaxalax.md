# What a menu knows about its contents

In *Video Game Menus*, RelaxAlax asks how an interface helps people find things while introducing the world they are about to enter. In *Game Menus Shouldn’t Be This Good*, he follows that question through a single system, the desk in **Kirby Air Riders**, and into its submenus. The studies below use the full English automatic caption tracks and decoded frames from both recordings. Dates, cuts, sound design arguments and host-made examples belong to their respective essays. [First essay, 13:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9AhDnRm5Xls) · [Kirby essay, 16:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mrGQhOqty6A)

### How the first essay tests its opening claim

RelaxAlax opens with an ice-cream cone. It holds something you want and it belongs to the experience of eating it. That is a more demanding brief than “make a menu look like the game.” His historical detour starts with games that had only a few choices, passes through crowded screens as the number of modes grew, and reaches games that reduce their front door to a few words beside a character. He misses some of the personality lost on that journey. He also admits he had to learn his way around *Persona 5*. Both observations matter for a first-time visitor. Give the next visitor a task they can find before they know the site's visual language. [Opening and scope](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9AhDnRm5Xls&t=34s) · [Minimalism and the Persona qualification](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9AhDnRm5Xls&t=382s)

An early example near 4:04 is *M.U.S.C.L.E.* The automatic track garbles the name. A second transcription of the audio and the photographed title screen resolve it. RelaxAlax describes looking for an early character select, which establishes why he included this game. Dating the first such screen would need additional historical evidence. [Title screen and the author's search](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9AhDnRm5Xls&t=238s)

Watch who is being chosen in *Mario Party*. At 4:34 the town offers an adventure or its rules. From 4:35 to 4:37 the camera approaches a green pipe, looks down inside, then meets Toad. By 4:38 the roster is on screen. The next prompt asks for **three computer players**. By 4:42 the party stands together outside another pipe. The landmark makes the cut legible. The change in the prompt explains why the game wants a second selection from the same cast. In a listening room where a visitor hosts a session and invites guests, show who owns the session before asking whom to invite. Let a returning host reopen the room directly. A repeated character animation supplies neither piece of information. [Town, pipe and two selection roles](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9AhDnRm5Xls&t=272s)

The middle of the essay moves from resemblance to action. A shelf can explain where books live. A playable hub goes further by letting the player use the game's own movement to find the next place. RelaxAlax recalls learning an object-based hub through a children's game and enjoys traversing levels to reach other levels. Later he uses *Splatoon* to complicate the distinction between a hub and a menu. For an archive, ask what someone learns on the walk. If the answer is the relationship between two photographs on a wall, preserve their positions when zooming out. If someone has a specific photograph's link, open it at the photograph. [Objects and playable hubs](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9AhDnRm5Xls&t=295s) · [Closing argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9AhDnRm5Xls&t=666s)

Two late examples reward checking the picture against the shorthand. In *Wandersong*, the author describes using directions. The framing at 9:34 has Credits above, Controls at left, Options at right and a last-save readout below. The Controls destination contains actual bindings and a way back. Four occupied edges do not establish four buttons. In *Sonic Adventure 2: Battle*, moving from Story to Stage Select changes the description before opening the map. The focused stage then supplies a name and grades. By 10:33 the map has moved from the ground to a station in space while the stage panel keeps its job. Give an audio archive a map if changing location reveals a real relationship between recordings. Keep the selected recording named when the map moves, and make save status readable without implying it can be pressed. The host eventually returns to food, his preference for the underlying game, and music over the outro. Those closing remarks qualify how much work a menu ought to demand of anyone. ### What the desk promises, and what it actually does

The opening joke is about a character stranded on a file-select island. Treat a menu as a real place and people start asking how things got there. The explanation of collecting upgrades and testing the resulting machine gives the desk an actual job. It has to organise different activities, settings and results. The sober opening primes the host for one tone, then the toy and stationery deliver another. Watch the cut at 2:37. The packaged figure is an editorial gag. At 2:41 we are back at the desk with City Trial selected. A portfolio can use a pinned contact sheet because it tells a photographer where the pictures live. A packaging gag supplies no evidence for putting an unskippable unboxing sequence on that route. [Opening and game premise](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mrGQhOqty6A) · [Toy, rules and return to the desk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mrGQhOqty6A&t=155s)

The stationery has surprisingly precise meanings. Under Rules, the selected tempo has a value and an explanation. On My Music the bars express **how often a track is picked**. Treating them as volume controls would change the setting the visitor thinks they are editing. As focus moves from Checklist to Miles Shop around 2:55–2:56, the brighter face changes owners while progress and balance remain visible. For a music site, put a “Playback frequency” label and the current value on the editable control. Leave the collection count attached to the collection even when another control receives focus. On a phone, tap once and check what changed. [Rules, music, progress and shop](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mrGQhOqty6A&t=164s)

The author uses calculators, bookshelves and instruments to explain why this desk feels familiar. Its strongest test is the route out of a solo space. At 4:11 Online Match is a focused desk choice. By 4:13 a handheld device fills the view. At 4:14 it offers Ranked, Quick and Paddock alongside mode information. Opening the device lets the player decide **how** to connect. On a shared conservation workbench, opening the correspondence tray should show the recipients and draft. Sending the invitation is a subsequent action. The author then asks the viewer to listen and describes how the same tune changes arrangement between modes. The essay cuts to black during the demonstration. Mode names and emblems carry the distinction for someone browsing in silence. [Solo desk to online choices](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mrGQhOqty6A&t=248s) · [Music discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mrGQhOqty6A&t=302s)

In the design-lineage section the host credits Michiko Sakurai as the UX designer, looks at the joining of scraps in *Smash 64*, and uses a modified camera to explore *Melee*. His reading of a virtual space behind the menu is conjecture. He values how few actions it takes to enter a *Brawl* mode even while admitting he can't explain some background marks. Later he praises a stat display spilling past its boundary as a way to convey excess. That flourish would cost a visitor something if it hid the actual total. On a product site, let an illustration overflow while the value and control remain fully readable. Finally, follow one selection. Around 10:50–10:52 the grid stays put as King Dedede gives way to Kirby and the description changes. At 11:14 Knuckle Joe is in the grid. At 11:15 the grid clears and the named rider appears small, then grows into position. Ready is still available separately. The cut to a larger image of Meta Knight's move just before this sequence is the editor's crop. It cannot establish an in-game zoom gesture or how soon the controls accept input. For a site that starts agents, moving focus can reveal an agent's capabilities. **Assign agent** puts that agent on the job. **Run job** starts the work. Check the queue after each of those three actions. In the final four minutes the creator gives channel members their own roster and voice callouts. Recognition has become the purpose of selection. The closing demonstration belongs to the creator and the automatic track is unreliable on individual members' names.
