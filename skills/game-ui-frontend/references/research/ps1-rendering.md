# Reading motion in Acerola’s PS1 study

Use this study when a brief calls for a PS1 look or an object that should feel tangible as the visitor explores it. Acerola builds a small scene in Godot and changes how it is drawn. The most revealing question for a website is **what should the visitor learn when the object moves?** A camera turn can expose a socket, the profile of a handle or the thickness of a record sleeve. That supplies a reason to move and a silhouette worth drawing.

## Direct the website from the brief

**A detail worth checking in the recreation.** The release shader rounds the dithered colour on a 0–255 scale, divides by eight and normalizes the result. The division uses floating point. For example, channel values 80 and 81 become 10 and 10.125 before normalization. That pass alone can retain intermediate shades between the nominal five-bit levels. If a brief asks for exact palette steps, inspect a rendered ramp. If it asks for the feel of printed or enamel art under a moving light, judge the object at its intended size and camera angle. [Release shader, lines 124–134](https://github.com/GarrettGunnell/Godot-PSX/blob/68c12457303daa1505f8636f00d593aab48a4f29/Shaders/playstation_one.gdshader#L124-L134)

In the narration, Acerola explains why many games showed less distortion than the checkerboard test. An oblique view exaggerates the effect. Designers could control the camera angle to keep a surface readable. For a product viewer, choose where the turn begins and ends. Let a label bend briefly as the side comes into view, then give the visitor a clear, front-facing reading position. [Camera discussion, 13:12–13:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y84bG19sg6U&t=792s)

Choose the cue the subject can actually reveal. For an object with a distinctive outline, start with a few well-placed vertices and a deliberate change of pose. For printed packaging, give its printed band enough surface area for the texture to shift on a turn. For an atmospheric location, choose an important foreground landmark before colouring the distance. Apply those choices to a bounded art layer. Keep the visible action, focus marker, prices and body copy sharp at the reader’s display size.

**Worked proposal.** A pedal maker asks visitors to compare three effects by ear. Compose one dark workbench with the selected pedal large enough to see its dials. Along its edge, show the three pedal names. Choosing one sets the name and active marker immediately. Its painted enclosure turns from a side view that exposes its sockets to a front view that reveals the dial layout. A coarse enamel stripe bends across one bevel during the turn. A single **Listen** button plays that pedal’s sample on request. Circuit details and clean product photos open on demand. On touch, the same names select each pedal. With reduced motion, swap directly to its front view. For three fixed poses, original pre-rendered images can carry the effect without a live 3D scene.

If the same maker asks for a repair-booking page, the visitor’s decision changes. Use a still view of the opened pedal beside the fault description and available appointment times. A moving enclosure earns space on the comparison page because it reveals construction. The repair appointment is settled by the symptoms and the date.
