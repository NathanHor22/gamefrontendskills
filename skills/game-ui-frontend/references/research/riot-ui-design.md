# How a game interface earns its motion

Riot’s *So You Wanna Make Games??* episode on user interface design gives the visual designer and the motion artist the same problem. A player wants to do something, needs certain facts at that moment, and has limited attention to spend finding them. The team can draw a remarkable control once it knows which decision the control serves. This study follows the published English captions and decoded picture through the 12:29 episode. The examples describe the versions shown in 2018. The theatre booking proposals are original. [Riot’s episode and teaching notes](https://www.riotgames.com/en/artedu/user-interface-design) · [Film](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw)

## Work out what the player needs before drawing the plate

**A map can occupy two different amounts of attention.** The episode first draws a pause-menu tree with separate routes to a saved game and the options. A second diagram lays out the playable view. A small map lives in the corner until the full map takes the centre. The blueprint returns to the playable view and then gains finished artwork. The UX work decides which information is one action away and where the player comes back. The UI work gives those states a recognisable visual relationship. On a theatre website, someone choosing a seat needs the stage direction and row beside the seat. Let them call up the whole auditorium to change section and return to the same row. Check that closing the map preserves their selection. [Information flow, 1:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=102s) · [Full map and finished HUD, 1:56–2:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=116s)

**The same Fortnite decision occupies different parts of a phone.** Senior visual designer Joy An asks where the player will be and what they need at the moment of input. The PC diagram has its map at top right and inventory along the lower right. The mobile diagram moves the map to top left, introduces a movement pad at lower left and puts separate round touch controls to the right. The scene remains visible between the controls. For a phone-sized seat plan, put the chosen places and the reservation control within reach of the tapping hand. Preserve the part of the plan that tells someone where those seats face. Test by actually selecting a pair with a thumb at the narrowest supported width. [PC, 3:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=201s) · [Mobile, 3:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=206s)

**The amount of interface follows the decision.** The film places card play, 4X and simulation screens beside a comparatively quiet narrative scene. The card player needs to compare choices before spending a turn. A large permanent overlay would get between a narrative player and the scene they are trying to read. A ticket buyer needs a dense comparison when searching for adjacent seats, then can give the view from one seat the whole stage. Test the transition by asking what additional fact the larger view reveals. [Card and strategy information, 3:39–3:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=219s) · [Narrative scene, 3:55–4:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=235s)

**A bar belongs to the thing drawn beside it.** In the proximity example, an enemy name and a red bar share a location. The next crop pairs a different bar with the player's level and XP. A bar has almost no meaning on its own. On a reservation page, a real expiry indicator belongs with the seats held under that reservation. A time strip beside the performance title could read as the length of the show. Ask a visitor what expires and what remains booked when that strip finishes. [Enemy and experience examples, 6:13–6:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=373s)

## Give the whole menu a visual language

In an illustrated comparison, the Crafting, Skill Tree and Quest windows originally put their headings, action controls and close controls in different places. By 4:59 all three titles occupy the top, the close controls sit at lower right and the active choices share a warm accent. The underlying arrangements still suit three different jobs. In the actual *League of Legends* client redesign, the team begins with Hextech. The presentation shows an acceptance control beneath a ring, icon construction, meanings for the circle, square and diamond, and easing curves alongside the type and colour decisions. This is a system for deciding what an unfamiliar screen means on first sight. [Three windows, 4:50–5:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=290s) · [League client development, 5:04–5:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=304s)

For the theatre, use a folding seat shape to identify a *place* on the floor plan, a stage-facing crop for a *view*, and the torn edge of a ticket for an actual *reservation*. The accessible-seating indicator belongs on the relevant places before and after selection. Let the selected seat acquire an additional contour. Giving an unavailable seat the same contour makes a different promise and is grounds to redraw it.

## Read the teaching graphics as closely as the game footage

**One theme can support several different jobs.** At 5:11 Riot shows a door, a gauntlet and a mechanical object as the starting imagery for Hextech. By 5:15 the presentation places an exploded ring drawing beside a *Match Found* state. A small, separate Accept control sits at the base of the ring. The next boards specify type hierarchy, icon construction on an 18 px grid, colour and shape. At 5:23 the shape board assigns structure to the square, focus to the circle and progression to the diamond. The labels explain why three related shapes exist. These are Riot's choices for this particular client. The deck shows a response to a match and a way to accept it. The illuminated ring alone establishes neither a countdown nor an acceptance gesture. [Object studies and match response, 5:10–5:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=310s) · [Type, icons and shape meanings, 5:18–5:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=318s)

**A player's eyes have a deadline.** In the competitive match at 6:33–6:38, champions and effects cross the centre. Team rosters keep their positions at the edges, the score is overhead and the map remains in the lower corner. The preceding health/XP comparison explains how to recognise a value. This clip adds the cost of *finding* it again while attention is elsewhere. It is broadcast footage, with viewer information around the match. Avoid inferring precisely what an individual competitor saw. For a site running agents live, keep the job identity and pause control in place while logs stream. Start two jobs, expand a log in one, then have the other fail. Can the operator identify and retry the failed job without losing their reading position in the first? [Competitive sequence and layout discussion, 6:27–7:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=387s)

**The sparkles are the wrong thing to take home.** At 7:39 a hand-drawn Play control already has a plate, verb and border. By 7:41 the same drawing has decorative glints. It is the episode's illustration of added polish. No pointer enters it. The actual *Into the Breach* clip later shows a pointer and a changing row. The difference matters when specifying a site's hover response. Give a held theatre ticket a readable seat number, a visible state and one next action. Temporarily remove the animated foil. If a viewer can no longer distinguish an inspected seat from one they have reserved, the missing piece is the reservation state. Glitter cannot supply it. [Drawn control, 7:39–7:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=459s) · [Pointer demonstration, 8:24–8:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=504s)

**Keep the diagram when the art arrives.** In the closing sketch a branching chart appears first. The UI artist joins the UX designer. At 9:56 some nodes become green circles, others red panels, while their connections remain visible. The narration adds a motion artist working across that same design. The lesson for a booking flow is to follow one real state through three revisions. Sketch inspect-seat, request-hold, accepted and unavailable. Give each a recognisable still shape. Finally decide which change needs movement, what starts it and what evidence completes it. Test with a slow or denied hold. Preview stays reversible, acceptance waits for the reservation response, and recovery returns to the correct row. [Sketch and handoff, 9:43–10:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=583s)

The second reading followed all 299 published English cues, from the introductory catalogue of game interfaces through the final advice on learning from mistakes. The speakers allow the same person to do UX and UI. They include print and packaging as examples of visual communication, question a single ideal layout and defend practice by reconstructing work one admires. The final interviews distinguish practising technique from solving an audience's actual problem. Taken together, their advice suggests a practical critique. Name the original difficulty, reconstruct the cue, change the subject matter, then put the difficulty back in front of a real visitor and see whether it still occurs. ## Play the work back to the team

Riot’s closing sketch gives three collaborators one evolving diagram. The UX designer maps information and paths. The visual designer defines the appearance of the connected screens. The motion artist reinforces the response at the point of action. In the interviews, designers describe recreating HUDs and menus to understand them and playing games to discover actual friction. An effective practice exercise is to reconstruct one observed gesture with simple art, vary the input, then redraw it in the subject’s own materials. [Workflow, 9:42–10:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=582s) · [Designers’ practice, 10:15–12:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sc3h5JXtIzw&t=615s)

**Critic’s round.** Give a visitor a phone and the brief “Find two adjacent places with a clear stage view.” After the first tap, ask which seat they are looking from and whether any seats are reserved. Have them inspect another row, return to the first, and try to hold the pair. Use the ticketing system’s actual result. With no booking integration, label the exercise as a sample and return a sample hold. If one place has become unavailable, mark that particular seat, preserve the rest of the visitor’s choices and offer another available pair. Run the same trip with a keyboard, no hover and reduced motion. A gorgeous fold that obscures the seat identity, claims a reservation on preview or waits for an animation before accepting a correction fails the brief.
