# What a title screen lets someone do before they start

NakeyJakey’s *The Art of Video Game Title Screens* asks what a player can learn in the interval between arriving and choosing to play. Its strongest examples offer different answers: understand the game’s objects, settle into its mood, see the consequence of a previous visit or try a small part of the experience. Read these as alternatives. Combining them all would make a visitor wait for the character of the site to emerge. [Video essay](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pi47bBT4G9Q)

## Follow what changes hands

**A folder can carry identity into the next decision.** At 5:06 four green *GoldenEye* dossiers carry clipped portraits. A red sight sits on one cover. Copy and Erase are separately labelled along the bottom. At 5:19 the edit moves to an open page with the portrait still attached. The red sight now belongs beside Select Mission, above Multiplayer. Its meaning survives the change of scale. Its target changes from a file to an action within that file. Jakey describes the later film-reel level selection and wonders aloud where title screen ends and main menu begins. The two close samples show the file and mission states. His superimposed pointing figure and the short cut between those states give no evidence of a pointer hover or of the original folder-opening duration. [File grid and mission page, 5:06–5:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pi47bBT4G9Q&t=306s)

For a concert photographer, pin one recognisable contact print to each band sleeve. Opening the sleeve carries that same print into a spread containing the nights shot with the band. The first tap changes scale and the available actions. A second tap opens that night. Put the band's name on the open spread so similar portraits and missing images still leave the visitor oriented. Test a direct link to a night, ordinary Back after entering through a sleeve, and a return after another band was previewed. Each route should reveal its actual band. A reticle copied onto every link would communicate none of this.

**A scene can tell time while the controls occupy the same place.** Compare the *Spec Ops: The Line* rooftop at 7:28, 7:40, 7:46 and 7:52. A bright flag, a standing figure and a darker fire-lit scene make the rooftop read differently across shots. Campaign stays highlighted at the right in the sampled views. Jakey attributes the tableaux to chapters and deliberately avoids narrating the later story. A montage does not give their unlock order. For an exhibition assembled over several visits, change the visible worktable only when the visitor actually adds a piece. A visitor midway through should see the pieces they placed. A fresh visitor should encounter the unassembled work without seeing the ending. Compare a fresh browser profile against a saved one, then open the same exhibit by URL in both. [Changing rooftops, 7:28–7:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pi47bBT4G9Q&t=448s)

**Who decides when the entrance is over?** *Braid* briefly writes a movement instruction directly across a playable scene. Jakey describes moving the character to begin. *Ocarina of Time* gives a different kind of time to the player. The early ridge grows lighter, the logo arrives, and the horse passes both distant and close behind that logo. In his account, waiting long enough brings on another cutscene. The close samples show riding beneath the title and later a red start prompt. They do not show what caused the change or supply an idle timeout. In a photo archive, give visitors two ways to enter. Selecting a hanging print can open the first photograph immediately, while someone who lingers can watch light move across the wall. Leave keyboard focus on one print through an entire ambient cycle, then press Enter. It should open the same print. ## What the speaker qualifies

Jakey's early arcade-to-PS1 chronology concerns a change of audience and where developers spent the spectacle. A coin-operated cabinet had to win a passer-by. Someone who bought a home game had already made that decision. He explicitly abandons a march-of-progress interpretation and introduces four overlapping lenses for his own examples. The extra “dog” category is a running joke. His enjoyment of *Bloodborne* survives a plain opening, and *The Last of Us* invites imagination largely by withholding information. His fondness for the sound of the *THPS2* wheel, leaving *Kingdom Hearts* playing and recalling the *Zelda* confirmation sound describes listening and memory. It supplies no reliable event timing. His *Halo 2* point is more specific than liking a score: the quiet recalls the reflective part of a combat game. ## Build the entrance from the work

**The first gesture can already do the work.** For a skate filmmaker’s archive, open on the actual contact sheet from a session. The first shot is a large playable frame. Give **Play the Southbank line** the profile of a wheel seen from the side. Let its rim roll a short distance around a fixed hub on focus. Activating the control plays that very line, with its skater, date and captions already available. *THPS2* supplies the relationship between object and choice. The sleeve, motion and film are the archive’s own.

**Decide what changes on a return visit.** For a three-part audio walk, keep the visitor’s last completed stop pinned on the opening route. Offer **Resume at the viaduct** and a way to revisit the start. Only a completed stop changes that marker. If someone can turn a little dial to hear a sample while browsing, keep the dial’s preview transient. Refresh, Back and another visitor opening a shared stop should each produce the appropriate state.

**Let the visitor set the pace.** An ambient opening to a field-recording collection might show reeds moving while the first named recording is already available. The visitor chooses whether to listen. Muting the site, requesting reduced motion or arriving via a direct recording link should all lead to the same usable collection. A second generic start prompt between the reeds and the recordings would repeat a choice the person already made.

**Critique the entrance on two visits.** Try acting immediately, then let the scene run before acting. Both routes should reach the same promised item. Turn off the sound, freeze the animated layer and try with a narrow viewport. The chosen object, available action and destination should still be identifiable. If the only memorable part is a movie that disappears before the first meaningful action, move its strongest object or gesture into that action.
